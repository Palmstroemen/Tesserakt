# The experiment behind the game {#experiment}

Kurz: Tesserakt is a game and an open experiment at the same time. From many anonymous games we want to find out whether horoscopes describe people better than chance.

## Our questions {#die_frage}

For thousands of years people have read meaning into the stars, numbers and calendars. Whether this yields more than a pleasing reflection has rarely been tested on many people at once. We go into this experiment without a ready-made answer and ask three questions. As soon as enough games have come together, we want to answer them with charts drawn from the collected data – perhaps already in a later edition of this book.

**First: do horoscopes hit more often than chance?** This is the basic question. Does a horoscope describe a person better when it knows their **real** date of birth than when it is given a random one? Because every horoscope is also calculated for many random birthdays for comparison, this can be tested for each tradition on its own. The answer may turn out differently for each horoscope.

**Second: does the season of birth play a part?** Even if the stars know nothing about us, the calendar might. A child born in spring has spent the months before in a different way from one born in autumn: in different light, with different food and the illnesses of a different season – and with a mother whose mood may have changed with winter and summer over the course of the pregnancy. If people rate themselves a little differently depending on their month of birth, this should show up in the data as a gentle wave across the year, whatever the horoscopes say. And it could explain why a horoscope hits now and then: whoever reads the calendar always reads the season as well.

**Third: are we shaped by the horoscope we grow up with?** Anyone who grows up in the Chinese cultural sphere often knows their animal sign from early childhood; in the West almost everyone knows their star sign. Whoever hears for years that Tigers are brave and Aries impatient may one day see themselves that way – or even become so. This is called a self-fulfilling prophecy. If it exists, the Chinese horoscope should describe people from the Chinese cultural sphere better than others, and Western astrology, in turn, people in the West. Our only clue is the rough region of the place of birth. It does not always tell where someone grew up – but across many games such an influence should still become visible.

## Why a single game is not enough {#ein_spiel_reicht_nicht}

A single result depends heavily on chance – on how you answer today, and on which of the seven horoscopes happens to fit. Only when many people play does this chance average out. Then it shows whether a horoscope does better on average than chance would lead us to expect.

## What we compare {#was_wir_vergleichen}

- **Your self-assessment:** 16 statements that you rate for yourself.
- **The horoscopes:** seven traditions, each calculated for your date of birth.
- **The control group:** the same horoscopes for 100 other, random birthdays. It shows how well a horoscope would fit "by chance".

## What happens to your data {#daten}

- You do not need an account.
- We store your date of birth, the time, your answers and the result. The apparatus never asks for your name.
- Of your place of birth we store only the time zone and a rough region (about 100 km).
- Only aggregated, anonymous analyses across many games are published.

## How honest is the self-assessment? {#selbsteinschaetzung}

Nobody sees themselves entirely objectively. That is not a flaw but part of the experiment: we compare the horoscopes with the picture people have of themselves. Because all horoscopes are measured against the same answers, the comparison is fair all the same.

## Join in {#mitmachen}

Every round is a small building block. If you share the game, the experiment gains in power – and perhaps you will discover along the way how differently the horoscopes see one and the same person.
